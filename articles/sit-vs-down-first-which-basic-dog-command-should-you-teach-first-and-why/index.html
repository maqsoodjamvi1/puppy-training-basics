When I bring home a new dog or start working with a fresh rescue client, the first question people usually ask is: “Should I teach ‘sit’ or ‘down’ first?”

It is a great question. Both are foundational obedience commands, and you will eventually need both. But after years of working with energetic puppies, stubborn adolescents, and nervous older dogs in living rooms and backyards across the country, I have a clear favorite.

If you want to set your dog up for fast success and keep your sanity intact, you should teach sit first. Here is why it works best as a starting point, how it changes your daily routine, and when you should transition to down.

Why “Sit” Wins the First-Place Ribbon

Teaching “sit” first comes down to biomechanics and psychology. Dogs naturally sit a lot. When they pause to look at you, wait for a door to open, or survey the room, their rear end often hits the floor on its own.

Because sitting is a natural posture, it is much easier to capture and reward than lying down. When you hold a treat right in front of a dog’s nose and slowly move it backward over their head, their nose goes up, their rear end goes down, and—boom—you have a sit.

It takes about ten seconds for most dogs to figure this out. That quick win builds confidence for the dog and gives the human handler an immediate sense of control. If you are feeling overwhelmed by a high-energy dog jumping all over your kitchen, getting that first “sit” gives you a tool you can use immediately to press the pause button.

The Problem With Starting With “Down”

While “down” is an essential command, starting with it often leads to frustration for both ends of the leash.

Lying flat on the floor requires a dog to make themselves vulnerable. If your dog is nervous, overly aroused, or unfamiliar with you, asking them to stretch out on their belly can actually trigger hesitation or outright refusal.

Furthermore, the physical lure for a “down”—taking a treat from the dog’s nose straight down to the floor between their front paws—often results in jumping, pawing, or frustration if the dog gets stuck halfway. They might crawl forward, bark, or try to snatch the treat instead of sliding into a proper down position.

Saving “down” for week two or three, once your dog already understands how to work for treats and trusts your hand movements, makes the process much smoother.

Real-World Scenarios Where “Sit” Saves the Day

The real power of teaching “sit” first is how easily it applies to everyday American household friction points. Here are a few ways you can use it starting today:

  • Doorway manners: Instead of letting your dog bolt through the front door when the mail carrier arrives or when it is time for a walk, ask for a sit. The door only opens when their bottom is glued to the floor.
  • Mealtime chaos: If your dog dances around while you are trying to fill their bowl, hold the bowl up, ask for a sit, and only lower the bowl when they comply. It teaches impulse control without needing a heavy-handed correction.
  • Greeting guests: When someone walks through your front door, a sitting dog cannot jump up and plant muddy paws on their chest.

In each of these scenarios, “sit” acts as a default behavior. Over time, your dog will start offering a sit automatically when they want something, replacing unwanted behaviors like whining or pawing.

How to Teach “Sit” in Three Simple Steps

You do not need fancy equipment to teach this—just a handful of your dog’s regular kibble or some small, soft training treats.

  1. Hold a treat right in front of your dog’s nose so they know you have it.
  2. Slowly move your hand straight back over their head toward their ears. As their nose follows the treat upward, their hips will naturally lower to the ground.
  3. The exact second their rear end touches the floor, say “Yes!” or click your clicker, and give them the treat.

Practice this three to five times in a row, once or twice a day. Keep sessions short so your dog does not get bored. Once your dog is following your hand motion reliably, you can add the verbal cue “Sit” right before you start moving your hand.

When to Move on to “Down”

Do not rush the process. Spend four to seven days mastering “sit” in low-distraction environments like your kitchen or living room before introducing “down.” Once your dog offers a sit quickly and reliably, you will have built the basic communication bridge needed to teach them to lie down with ease.
