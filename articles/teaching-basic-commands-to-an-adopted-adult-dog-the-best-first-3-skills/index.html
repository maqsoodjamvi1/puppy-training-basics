Bringing home an adopted adult dog is an incredible feeling, but walking through the front door for the first time is also a bit surreal. Unlike a puppy who grows up learning your household rules from day one, an adult dog arrives with a fully formed personality, a past you likely know very little about, and zero understanding of your specific routine.

When I brought home my rescue mix, Buster, I made the mistake of trying to teach him five different things during his first weekend. He looked at me with a mix of exhaustion and total confusion. Adult dogs do not need a crash course in advanced obedience right away. They need clarity, predictability, and a few high-value tools to help them navigate their new human world safely.

If you are starting fresh with an adult rescue, skip the complex tricks for now. Here are the three most practical skills to teach first, and how to actually get them done in real life.

1. The “Look at Me” Cue (Building Basic Focus)

Before your dog can sit, stay, or drop anything, they have to be able to look at you. In a brand-new home, your dog is bombarded with unfamiliar sights, sounds, and smells. If their attention is locked onto the window or a noise in the hallway, your voice is just background noise.

The “Look at Me” (or “Watch Me”) cue establishes a communication channel. You do not need hours of formal practice for this; you can do it right in your living room.

  • Hold a smelly, high-value treat (like a tiny piece of plain cooked chicken or soft cheese) right between your fingers.
  • Bring the treat up to the bridge of your own nose, right between your eyes.
  • The second your dog’s eyes lock onto your face—even if it is just for a split second—say “Yes!” and immediately hand over the treat.
  • Practice this five to ten times in a row, once or twice a day.

Once your dog realizes that looking at your face unlocks good things, you can start adding the verbal cue “Look” right before you bring the treat to your eyes. This is your ultimate safety net when you are out on a walk and need to redirect your dog away from a distraction before they react.

2. A Reliable “Sit” for Real-World Impulse Control

Teaching a sit to an adult dog isn’t about looking impressive for guests. It is a practical tool for daily management. An adopted dog who knows how to sit automatically when faced with an exciting situation is much easier to live with.

Instead of treating “sit” as a trick performed on a living room rug, integrate it into high-friction daily moments. For example, use the sit cue before you clip on the leash for a walk, before you put their food bowl down on the floor, or before you open the front door to go outside.

Hold a treat just above your dog’s nose and slowly move it backward over their head toward their ears. As their nose follows the treat upward, their rear end will naturally lower to the ground. The moment their bottom touches the floor, mark the behavior with “Yes!” and deliver the food.

If you adopted a large or stiff-jointed adult dog, do not force them into a long hold. Keep training sessions under three minutes. The goal is building a habit of polite pausing, not creating a military-style drill sergeant routine.

3. A Stress-Free “Leave It”

Of all the commands you can teach, a solid “Leave It” is the one that will genuinely keep your adopted dog out of danger. Adult rescue dogs often have a habit of scavenging—whether they are eyeing a dropped pill on the bathroom floor, a suspicious piece of trash on the sidewalk, or your favorite shoe.

Do not practice this with things your dog genuinely values right out of the gate. Start low and slow in a quiet room.

  • Hold a piece of kibble in a closed fist and present your fist to your dog. They will likely sniff, lick, or paw at your hand.
  • Ignore the behavior entirely. Say nothing. Wait until your dog pulls their head or nose away from your hand, even by a millimeter.
  • The exact millisecond they back off, say “Yes!” and give them a different, better treat from your other hand. Never let them eat the actual piece of food they were trying to get from your fist, or they will learn that “leave it” just means “wait a second before eating.”
  • Gradually progress to putting the food on the floor under an open hand, then under a shoe, and finally out in the open on the ground.

When practiced consistently for just a few minutes a day over a couple of weeks, this command stops being a battle of wills and becomes a reflex.

Training an adopted adult dog is a marathon, not a sprint. Do not stress if your new companion doesn’t master these skills overnight. Consistency, patience, and high-value rewards will bridge the gap between their past and their bright future with you.
