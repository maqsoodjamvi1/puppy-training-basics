If you brought home a puppy recently, your favorite pair of sweatpants and the wooden legs of your coffee table are probably already covered in tiny, sharp tooth marks. It is completely normal to feel a mix of total adoration and sheer panic when your living room starts looking like a beaver habitat. When I brought my Labrador mix home, I thought I was doing everything right until I caught him gnawing on the living room baseboards while I was making coffee.

Puppy biting is rarely about pure destruction; it is how babies explore their world, soothe sore gums during teething, and burn off excess energy. Stopping the damage to your home and wardrobe does not require yelling or harsh punishments. It requires changing the environment, managing your puppy’s access, and teaching them what is actually okay to sink their teeth into.

Redirection Beats Reprimands Every Time

When you see your puppy latching onto the hem of your jeans or chewing the arm of the sofa, your first instinct is probably to shout “no!” and pull them away. From the puppy’s perspective, this often backfires. They hear a loud noise, get a sudden burst of attention, and realize that biting your clothes is a fantastic way to make you play with them.

Instead of getting into a tug-of-war with your belongings, keep a stash of approved chew toys within arm’s reach of your usual sitting spots. When your puppy clamps onto your shoe or shirt sleeve, do not make eye contact or yell. Quietly slip a durable toy, like a rubber chew ring or a fleece tug rope, right into their mouth. The second they let go of your property and grip the toy, praise them softly.

Management Is Your Best Defense

You cannot watch a puppy twenty-four hours a day, and expecting them to make the right choice when left unattended in a furnished room is setting them up to fail. Until your puppy learns house rules, your job is to remove temptation entirely.

Put away your nice shoes, keep laundry off the floor, and use baby gates to block access to carpeted rooms or expensive furniture when you cannot supervise directly. If you are sitting on the couch and need to answer an email or cook dinner, put your puppy in a secure exercise pen with a few interactive toys.

  • Use bitter spray wisely: Safe, non-toxic deterrent sprays can make wooden chair legs and baseboards taste intensely bitter, instantly ruining the appeal of your furniture.
  • Create a designated zone: Keep a specific basket of toys in every room you frequent so you never have to scramble across the house to find a redirection item.
  • Inspect your own wardrobe: Avoid wearing loose, flowing bathrobes or fringed blankets around a teething puppy, as these mimic the exact texture of a tug toy.

Fulfill Their Need to Chew Legitimately

Puppies have a biological drive to chew, especially between the ages of four and six months when their adult teeth are pushing through their gums. If you do not give them an acceptable outlet for this urge, they will find one on their own—usually your favorite armchair.

Give your puppy long-lasting chew items that are specifically rated for their current size and chewing style. Stuffed rubber Kong toys filled with plain yogurt, pumpkin, or wet dog food and then frozen can keep a puppy busy for twenty minutes while soothing sore gums. Rotate their toys every few days so old items feel new and exciting again, which naturally draws their attention away from household items.

Address Over-Excitement and Fatigue

Have you ever noticed that your puppy turns into a tiny land shark right around seven o’clock in the evening? This usually happens because they are overtired and overstimulated. Just like human toddlers, puppies act out and bite everything in sight when they desperately need a nap.

If your puppy starts attacking your ankles, jumping on the couch cushions, and ignoring your redirection, they probably need enforced crate time or quiet time in a pen. A puppy who gets sixteen to eighteen hours of sleep a day is far less likely to destroy your home than one running on empty adrenaline.

Protecting your belongings while raising a puppy is a marathon of consistency rather than a quick fix. By setting up your home to prevent mistakes, keeping alternative chew items close by, and recognizing when your puppy needs rest, you can save your furniture and your sanity. As you figure out the daily routine that works best for your household, taking a look at structured training resources can give you even more confidence for the weeks ahead.
