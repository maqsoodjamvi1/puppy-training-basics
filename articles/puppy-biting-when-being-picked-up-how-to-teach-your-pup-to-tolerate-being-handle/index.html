There is a specific kind of heartbreak that happens when you reach down to scoop up your new puppy for a cuddle, and instead of a happy greeting, you are met with snapping teeth and angry growling. You probably pulled your hands back instantly, feeling a mix of frustration, confusion, and worry. Is my puppy aggressive? Am I doing something wrong?

When I brought my last rescue puppy home, I faced this exact hurdle. Every time my hands approached his midsection, he turned into a tiny, squirming alligator. The good news is that this behavior is rarely a sign of true aggression. Most of the time, it is simply a mix of overstimulation, a startled startle reflex, and a total lack of understanding about what being lifted actually means to a dog.

To help your puppy feel safe and cooperative, you need to change how handling feels from the ground up. Here is the step-by-step approach I used to teach my own pup to tolerate—and eventually accept—being picked up.

Figure Out Why Your Puppy is Biting

Before you can fix the biting, you need to look at the context. Puppies rarely bite just to be mean; they bite because they are communicating.

If your puppy lunges when you reach for them during a late-night play session, they are likely overtired and overstimulated. Think of them like a cranky toddler who throws a tantrum right before crashing for a nap. If they bite when you lift them off the couch, they might be startled by a sudden loss of footing or an awkward grip that puts pressure on their ribs or tummy.

Watch your puppy’s body language right before your hands touch them. Are their eyes wide? Is their body stiff? Identifying these micro-triggers helps you adjust your approach so you aren’t accidentally scaring them every time you need to move them.

Break the Pick-Up Down into Tiny Steps

Most of us make the mistake of treating “picking up a puppy” as one single action. To a dog weighing five pounds, being hoisted into the air is like a sudden amusement park drop ride. Instead of doing it all at once, break the process down into discrete, manageable steps.

Start when your puppy is calm—perhaps right after a nap, rather than in the middle of a wrestling match.

  1. The Hover: Place your hands a few inches away from your puppy without touching them. Say a cue word like “Up?” If they stay calm, immediately reward them with a high-value treat, like a tiny piece of boiled chicken.
  2. The Touch: Gently place your hands on their sides for one second, remove them, and reward.
  3. The Pressure: Apply very light pressure, as if you are preparing to lift, but keep their paws firmly on the floor. Reward.
  4. The Inch: Lift their front paws off the ground for a fraction of a second, leaving their back feet down. Reward generously.

By breaking the sequence apart, your puppy’s brain has time to process that your hands mean good things are coming, rather than an imminent loss of control.

Manage Your Grip and Mechanics

How you hold your puppy matters immensely. Many puppies bite because our standard lifting technique feels deeply unnatural or painful to them.

Avoid grabbing your puppy around the scruff or lifting them straight up by their armpits, which puts unnatural strain on their shoulder joints. Instead, use a two-handed cradle method. Slide one hand under their chest just behind their front legs, and use your other hand to scoop up their hind end, keeping their body pressed gently against your torso for security.

If your puppy feels fully supported and stable, their panic response drops significantly. A secure puppy is much less likely to thrash and bite.

Use Food to Redirect and Build Positive Associations

If your puppy’s immediate reflex is to mouth your hands, keep a stash of tiny treats ready before you even bend down.

When you need to pick your puppy up to move them off the rug or put them in their crate, offer a long, flat treat or a lick of a high-value paste right at their nose level as you lift. While their mouth is busy licking or chewing, they physically cannot bite your hands. This creates a positive distraction and pairs the sensation of being lifted with an incredible reward. Over time, the physical act of being picked up becomes a predictor of good things, rewriting their initial fearful or defensive reaction.

Handling your puppy safely and calmly is an ongoing project that requires patience, especially during their sharp-toothed development stages. Taking things slow now pays off massively when your dog is full-grown and you need to lift them into the car or onto a vet’s examination table without a struggle.
