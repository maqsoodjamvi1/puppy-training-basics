The second the deadbolt turns at my front door, the chaos begins. If you are living with a young puppy, you probably know this drill intimately. The second a guest steps inside, your sweet little dog turns into a tiny, spinning shark, alternating between leaping up at waist height and clamping onto shoelaces, panting jacket hems, or bare ankles.

It is equal parts embarrassing and painful. When friends come over, I used to find myself doing an awkward chicken dance—holding grocery bags in one hand, trying to fend off razor-sharp teeth with the other, and shouting apologies about how “he’s usually so sweet.”

If you are at your wit’s end trying to manage a puppy who views every visitor as a chew toy, you are not alone. Stopping this frantic greeting behavior requires changing how you manage arrivals, setting boundaries before the door even opens, and teaching your puppy that calm behavior gets them what they want, while teeth on skin makes the fun instantly stop.

Why Your Puppy Turns Into a Biting Piranha When People Arrive

To fix the door-greeting frenzy, it helps to understand why your puppy does it in the first place. When you walk through the door after being gone, or when a guest arrives, your puppy experiences a massive spike in adrenaline and excitement. They have been alone or resting, and suddenly the environment is energized.

Puppies explore the world with their mouths. When they get over-aroused, their impulse control goes straight out the window. They want to interact with the new human, but they do not yet know how to channel that huge burst of energy. Biting and jumping become their default physical release for sheer excitement.

If you push your puppy away, squeal, or wave your hands around, your puppy thinks you are playing a high-stakes game of tug-of-war with their favorite toy: you. To them, your reaction is an invitation to bite harder.

Step One: Change the Doorbell Routine

The biggest mistake we make is treating the doorbell or the turning door handle as the cue for the chaos to start. Puppies are brilliant pattern-recognition machines. If every time the bell rings, you rush to the door in a panic and the puppy goes wild, hearing that sound now triggers an automatic excitement loop in their brain.

You need to desensitize your puppy to the triggers of a visitor arriving:

  • Grab your keys, jingle them, and then sit back down on the couch. Do this repeatedly until your puppy stops reacting to the sound.
  • Walk over to the front door, touch the handle, and turn it without opening it. If your puppy stays calm, toss a treat on the floor behind them.
  • Practice opening the door an inch, and if your puppy rushes forward, shut it immediately. Repeat this until they learn that staying behind an imaginary boundary line is the only way the door actually opens.

By breaking down the arrival sequence into boring, bite-sized pieces, you take the electricity out of the doorbell.

Step Two: Use Management Instead of Punishment

Until your puppy learns better habits, stop setting them up to fail. When you know a delivery driver is coming or a friend is dropping by, do not let your puppy have free run of the entryway.

Put your puppy on a house leash—a light, six-foot leash with the loop cut off so it does not snag on furniture—or place them behind a baby gate in the hallway. This gives you physical control without needing to grab their collar, which often escalates the biting.

If a guest comes over, have them ignore the puppy completely for the first two minutes. No eye contact, no high-pitched baby talk, and no reaching down to pet. Let the guest stand like a boring statue. Once your puppy realizes this new human is not going to engage with their frantic jumping and biting, the adrenaline starts to drain. Only when all four paws are on the floor and the mouth is closed should your guest turn, calmly acknowledge the puppy, and offer a gentle scratch under the chin.

Step Three: Teach the Alternative Behavior

Dogs do not just stop doing bad things because we want them to; they stop because a better, more rewarding option takes its place. Instead of teaching your puppy “no bite” while they are mid-air, teach them what to do instead when someone walks in.

I like to use a specific mat placed ten feet away from the front door.

  • Toss a handful of high-value treats onto the mat.
  • Teach your puppy that the mat is the magic zone where good things happen.
  • Practice sending your puppy to the mat before you open the door.

If the puppy breaks position and runs to the door to jump and bite, the door closes immediately. The guest steps back outside, and the fun stops. We only reopen the door when the puppy relocates back to the mat. It takes patience and a lot of repetition, but eventually, the puppy makes the connection: calm on the mat equals the door opening; teeth on skin equals the door closing.

Fixing door-greeting behavior takes consistency from everyone in your household and any regular visitors you host. If one person lets the puppy jump and mouth, you have to start back at square one. Take a breath, set up your management tools before the next guest arrives, and focus on rewarding calm choices over chaotic ones.
